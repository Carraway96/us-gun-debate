Rights · Safety · Responsibility

One country, different rules

Federal laws apply across the US. States also have their own gun laws. [2] This means a debate can take place at more than one level: should Congress act for the whole country, or should state lawmakers decide? Before judging an argument, identify who would make the rule and where it would apply.

A protected right

The Second Amendment protects an individual right to own a gun. That right has legal limits. [1] This makes the American debate partly a constitutional debate. People disagree about public safety, but they also disagree about the limits of government power. A policy can raise questions about both its practical effects and its legal justification.

More than a yes or no

Someone can support gun ownership and still support particular safety rules. Equally, someone may accept a safety goal but oppose the way a proposal tries to achieve it. Avoid dividing everyone into two fixed teams. Ask what each person supports, what they oppose and why. Agreement on one measure does not imply agreement on every other measure.

01 / Explore

What is the disagreement?

4 themes · 8 arguments

Here, gun control means rules about who can buy, own, store or carry guns. “For” means supporting stricter rules; “against” means opposing additional restrictions. It does not mean supporting or opposing all gun ownership.

Start with a specific policy

“Stricter gun laws” can describe very different ideas. A background check asks whether a buyer is legally eligible. A storage rule concerns access to a gun that is already owned. A licensing proposal creates a process for permission. Each affects different people in different ways. An argument about one measure may not apply to another.

Why can people disagree so strongly?

The debate combines several values: personal freedom, protection from violence, responsibility and trust in government. Two people may both want a safer community but disagree about what creates safety. One may put greater trust in shared rules. Another may put greater trust in the individual’s ability to respond to danger. Neither starting point removes the need for evidence.

American experiences also differ. Pew’s 2023 survey found that gun ownership was more common in rural areas than in urban areas, and more common among Republicans and Republican-leaning adults than among Democrats and Democratic-leaning adults. [4] These are patterns in a survey, not rules about every individual. Do not assume a person’s opinion from their address or political party.

Read for three different kinds of statement

A factual claim describes something that can be checked. A value judgement says what matters or what is fair. A prediction says what a policy might change. For example, saying that a right is important expresses a value; saying a new rule will reduce injuries makes a prediction. Strong reasoning shows how these statements connect.

02 / Think, pair, discuss

Can you argue the other side?

Choose one specific proposal: wider background checks, safe-storage rules or a gun licence. Write it in one sentence so that everyone is discussing the same measure. Decide which level of government would introduce it and who would be affected. Your task is to explain a position fairly before deciding whether you agree.

01

Build your case

Choose an argument and state its main claim in your own words. Explain the steps connecting the proposal to the result you expect. Add a relevant source and say what it actually shows. If your argument depends on a value, such as freedom or fairness, name that value openly. Finish by identifying one weakness in your own case.

02

Switch perspectives

Give the strongest reasonable objection, even if you disagree with it. Explain what the other side wants to protect. Avoid replacing its argument with an extreme version that is easy to attack. Ask your partner, “Would someone who holds this view recognise my explanation?” Then identify one point both sides could accept.

03

Make a judgement

Compare the arguments for this proposal. Consider the quality of the evidence, the size of any likely benefit and the burdens involved. You may support the rule, reject it or suggest changes. Explain why your conclusion follows from your reasons. State what new information could make you change your mind.

Present a claim: “Supporters argue that… because…”
Use evidence: “This source examines… It supports the claim that…”
Identify a limit: “However, it does not tell us whether…”
Respond fairly: “A reasonable objection is… My response would be…”
Reach a conclusion: “On balance, I support / oppose this proposal because…”
Find common ground: “A possible compromise would be…, provided that…”

A short writing task

Write 180–250 words answering: Should the US introduce your chosen proposal? Begin by defining the proposal. Develop one argument in favour and one against. Use at least one source carefully, including a limitation. End with your own reasoned judgement. You do not have to find both arguments equally convincing, but you must explain both accurately.

Before handing it in, underline your claim, circle the evidence and mark your response to the opposing argument. Check that you have explained the connection between the evidence and the conclusion. A source name on its own is not an explanation.

Questions to take the debate further
  1. Rights and limits: When can a safety rule justify limiting an individual right? Suggest a principle you would also apply to a right you personally value.
  2. National or state rules: Should every state follow the same minimum standard? Explain a possible benefit of consistency and a possible benefit of local choice.
  3. Measuring success: What would show that your proposal works? Distinguish injuries, crime, costs, public confidence and legal rights.
  4. Unequal effects: Could the same requirement create different burdens for different people? Suggest a change that might reduce those differences.
  5. Changing your mind: What finding would challenge your position? If no possible evidence could affect it, is your view mainly a value judgement?
  6. Compromise: Can you design a proposal that protects the other side’s main concern? Explain what you would keep, change and give up.

Discuss policies respectfully. You do not need to share personal experiences of violence. Challenge a claim or a reason rather than making assumptions about the person presenting it.

03 / Language toolkit

Words that unlock the debate

Select a term for an explanation and guidance on using it in an argument. Start with the words you need for your chosen theme. Then try explaining one term to a partner without reading the definition. Accurate vocabulary makes it easier to disagree precisely and fairly.

04 / Check the evidence

Follow the source, not just the claim.

These sources have different jobs. A court decision explains the law; a survey measures opinions; research examines outcomes. None can answer every question. Choose the source that matches the claim you are making. The notes below explain what each source can help you investigate and what it cannot establish on its own. You can write a strong argument while openly recognising uncertainty.

Read the original court decisions

District of Columbia v. Heller (2008) ↗ · United States v. Rahimi (2024) ↗

These are court texts hosted by Cornell’s Legal Information Institute. They are more demanding than the classroom reading. Heller addresses the individual right; Rahimi shows how the Court considered a particular temporary restriction related to a credible threat. Neither decision is a general approval or rejection of every gun law.

A quick source check
  1. Identify the author and purpose. Is the source explaining a procedure, interpreting the law, measuring opinion or examining outcomes? Each task needs a different kind of expertise.
  2. Check the date. Say when a survey took place or when an analysis was published. An older source may be useful, but it should not be presented as a description of today without checking.
  3. Match the claim. Find the passage that supports your statement. Does it concern the same policy, population and outcome? Do not stretch a narrow result into a general conclusion.
  4. Look for alternatives. Could another factor help explain a pattern? What additional evidence would make the explanation stronger? Be especially careful when comparing places with many differences.
  5. State the limit. Complete this sentence: “This source helps show…, but it does not establish…” This improves your argument by making its boundaries clear.

Practise: Take one sentence from your draft. Identify whether it is a fact, a prediction or a value judgement. Find a suitable source if needed, then rewrite the sentence so that its certainty matches the evidence.
